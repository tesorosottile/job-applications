Dear RBB Economics,

Competition work appeals to me for a specific reason: the answer usually turns on which of
two plausible mechanisms is actually driving what you observe, and that distinction has to
be defended with data rather than asserted.

That is the problem my Master's thesis was built around. I used Germany's April 2023 nuclear
phase-out as a natural experiment, assembled roughly 140,000 observations from ENTSO-E,
Network Transparency and futures market data, and found no aggregate effect on renewable
dispatch — but a significant increase in onshore wind generation and higher day-ahead prices.
Two explanations competed: a reduced market value of renewables, or reduced grid congestion.
I separated them using interacted regression models rather than picking the one that told a
neater story. I wrote the propensity score matching and OLS routines myself in Python instead
of calling a library I could not inspect.

Since then I have spent a year at a consultancy carrying the commercial strategy for one
product on my own — around €34,000 in average monthly net sales, with second-year unit sales
above the first in a crowded market. The instructive part was that revenue slipped while
volume rose. The useful answer was pricing dynamics, and the work was reporting that to
studio CEOs rather than the headline number they were hoping for.

I am 24 and a year into my career, so I am not going to claim depth I do not have. What I
would point to is that both of those pieces of work were mine end to end, and both came down
to defending a mechanism to people who wanted a different answer.

I work in English and Italian; my German is B1 and improving, which is why I am applying to
RBB generally rather than to one office.
